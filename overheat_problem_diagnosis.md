# OVERHEAT: Why the Current Math Feels Predictable and Dead

A problem diagnosis, grounded in the validator output (game modes, distribution bands, and Plasma mode statistics). This document lists problems only, not fixes. The theme is strong. The math underneath is working against everything that makes a burst game compulsive.

## The core truth these problems violate

Compulsive play does not come from the big win. It comes from the reinforcement schedule: frequent, unpredictable, small rewards that keep the hand on the button, with the rare huge win as the story the player tells themselves about why they are there. The current distribution has inverted that schedule. Almost every problem below is a symptom of the same disease.

---

## 1. The 1x to 40x range is empty (the fatal one)

For Plasma, every payout band from (1, 2) up through (50, 100) has a count of zero. A player cannot win 2x, 3x, 5x, 10x, 20x, or 50x. Those outcomes do not exist in the game. The only things that can happen are: nothing (89.5 percent), a partial refund below stake (9.7 percent), or a jackpot around 130x and up (0.9 percent).

The entire zone of small and medium wins, the zone that produces the drip that keeps a person seated, has been deleted. What remains is the least addictive reinforcement schedule that exists: rare, large, and fixed in feel. Variable reward with frequent small hits produces compulsion. Sparse large hits produce quitting.

## 2. Only six possible outcomes, so the game is solved in one session

Number of unique payout values for Plasma: 6. Within twenty or thirty rounds a player has seen the complete vocabulary of what the game can do. There is no "what if this time," because the player already knows this time can only be one of six things, and five of them are bad.

Unpredictability is the oxygen of these games, and a six value outcome space suffocates it. The brain finishes mapping the distribution almost immediately, novelty dies, and compulsion dies with it. This is exactly the predictability that needs to be avoided, and it is measurable right here.

## 3. Profit is 116 spins away on average

For Plasma, the average gap between profitable outcomes is 116 spins, and the average gap between any win at all is 10 spins. At five seconds a round, a player grinds roughly ten minutes of continuous loss before a profitable outcome arrives on average. The worst case loss streak is 796 spins for one in a thousand players.

No one survives that. The chase only works when profit feels recent and attainable, so the player believes the next one is close. When profit is a distant, abstract event, the player does not chase, they leave. The cadence is punishing rather than teasing.

## 4. The sub bet refunds are frustration events wearing a win costume

The 9.7 percent "sub bet" slice is payouts between zero and stake, effective values around 0.05x and 0.5x. Mechanically the counter ticks up, so the game codes it as a win, but the player gets back less than they put in. Psychologically that reads as a loss, and a specific kind: the rig mined some coins, the player watched them accumulate, then it fried below break even and handed back scraps.

It is the game showing profit and taking it away, without the payoff of a genuine near miss. Frequent small losses dressed as wins erode trust faster than clean losses do, because they feel like being toyed with.

## 5. There is no near miss, and near miss is the strongest hook in the genre

The most powerful moment in crash is watching the climb approach the target and dying one notch short. It fires the reward circuit like a win but lands as a loss, which produces the "so close, one more" compulsion that funds the whole session. For that to exist you need a crash point that is a smooth continuous value and a target the player can watch it approach.

The current build has neither. With six discrete outcomes there is no 38x when the player wanted 40x. The rig either vanishes early or prints 130x. The heartbreak that would keep people in the seat has no way to occur. This is not a tuning gap, it is a missing mechanic.

## 6. The outcome is sealed at play, so the player has no agency

This is the deep structural problem and it is easy to miss. Real crash is addictive largely because the player owns the cash out timing. When they bust, it was their decision to push, so they blame themselves, and self blame is what generates "I will do better next time."

The current outcome is decided the instant play returns, and the reveal just animates a fixed result. The player is a spectator of a sealed number. That converts every loss from "I got greedy" into "the machine decided I lose," which produces resentment and exit, not the productive self blame loop. Right now this is a slot wearing a crash skin. The temperature limit chosen up front is an auto cash out, which is better than nothing, but if the whole round is pre decided and merely plays back, the live tension of bank now or push one more degree never happens, and that live tension is the product.

## 7. The experience is bimodal with no gradient, which reads as cold or rigged

86.85 percent of the return sits in a single band around 130x at roughly one in 118. The game's entire meaning is gated behind one rare event, and nothing else moves the needle. The felt experience is a long flat nothing, then a spike, then a long flat nothing.

There is no warming up, no sense of building toward something, no feedback gradient that tells the player they are close or getting closer. Good volatility has texture: a ramp of small then medium then occasionally large that lets the player feel rhythm and momentum. This has two states, dead and jackpot, and the human pattern matcher labels that as a cold or dishonest machine.

## 8. The eleven rigs are the same game in different costumes

Every mode is the identical shape, dead then sub bet then rare spike, scaled to a different cap and hit rate, all pinned to 96.5 percent RTP. Because RTP is invariant across them, choosing a rig changes only variance, never expected result.

Mathematically that is elegant. Experientially it is flat. The choice of rig is a volatility slider with eleven fancy names. There is no strategic interaction between rig choice and anything else, no reason to switch mid session beyond boredom, and no rig that plays differently in kind rather than degree. A player finds the variance they like and ignores the other ten. That is breadth pretending to be depth.

## 9. Nothing carries between rounds, so there is no reason to come back

Each round is independent and sealed, and there is no meta layer over the top. No personal best multiplier to beat, no streak that builds toward a bonus, no escalating pot, no unlock, no collectible, no run that a bust ends.

Addictive designs put a goal above the per round randomness so that even losing rounds feel like progress toward something, which keeps the session alive through cold streaks and gives a reason to return tomorrow. This is eleven disconnected modes with no connective tissue. The cold streaks have nothing to sustain the player, and there is no hook that survives the player closing the tab.

## 10. The reveal telegraphs itself and collapses tension halfway through

With six outcomes and a sealed result, the animation can only be one of a small number of scripts, and those scripts correlate with the outcome. A player quickly learns that the fast fry animation means a dud, and the moment it starts they disengage for the rest of the reveal.

Tension in these games has to be preserved until the final possible instant. A tiny, correlated set of reveal scripts cannot hold uncertainty, so the dread evaporates early and the five seconds becomes a countdown to a result the player already read.

## 11. The theme promises a fantasy the math refuses to deliver

The fiction is push a rig hot, watch coins pile up, bank them before it melts. That promises escalation the player controls, rising tension, and a greed versus caution tradeoff that is theirs to make. The math delivers a coin flip into a jackpot with no build, no control, and no tradeoff.

Players feel the gap between what the skin promises and what the numbers do, even if they cannot articulate it, and a skin that lies about the game underneath reads as cheap. The costume is excellent. It is currently draped over a mechanic that does none of what the costume implies.

---

## The single thread

The distribution is a coarse, top heavy, sealed lookup with an empty middle, and the mechanic gives the player nothing to do. Predictability, sparse rewards, no near miss, no agency, no depth, no meta, all fall out of those two facts.
